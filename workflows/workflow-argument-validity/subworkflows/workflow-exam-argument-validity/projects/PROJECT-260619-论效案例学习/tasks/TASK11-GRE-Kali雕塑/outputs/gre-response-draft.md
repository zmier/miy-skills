The prediction that the life-size Kalinese sculptures will decline in value while the miniature sculptures will increase in value depends on several questions that must be answered before the argument can be evaluated.

First, it is necessary to know whether the recently discovered molds are sufficient evidence for the production method of the entire life-size sculptures. The discovery of molds for heads and hands may show that artists used molds for some parts of some sculptures, but it does not necessarily show that whole life-size figures were made from actual body molds. If the molds were limited to certain body parts, then the argument's claim about the general production method of life-size sculptures would be much weaker.

Second, we need to know how the molds were used. The argument assumes that the molds were the main production method rather than practice tools, reference tools, or aids used together with carving skill. If artists used molds only as an initial guide and then relied heavily on carving techniques to complete the sculptures, the discovery would not undermine the artistic skill involved in making the life-size figures. In that case, the predicted loss of value would be less plausible.

Third, the argument requires information about whether the life-size and miniature sculptures are comparable objects. The miniatures may differ in style because they were made in a different period, for a different purpose, from different materials, or within a different artistic tradition. If so, their abstract style would not be explained by the impossibility of using body molds. This would weaken the claim that the discovery makes miniatures relatively more valuable.

Fourth, it is important to ask whether the scarcity of carving tools actually indicates that such tools were not used. Ancient tools may have been made of perishable materials, removed from the island, reused, or simply not yet discovered. If tool preservation or discovery is incomplete, the absence of many tools cannot strongly support the claim that the life-size sculptures were not carved with tools and skill.

Finally, the prediction about market value depends on how collectors determine value. If collectors care mainly about rarity, age, beauty, historical significance, or cultural importance, then the discovery of molds may not reduce the value of life-size sculptures. Similarly, miniature sculptures will increase in value only if collectors respond to the discovery by valuing their technique or artistic independence more highly. Without evidence about collector preferences and market behavior, the price prediction is speculative.

Thus, the argument can be evaluated only after these questions are answered. The answers would show whether the mold discovery truly changes our understanding of the sculptures' production methods and whether that changed understanding would actually affect the market value of either type of sculpture.
